An Imagined Lord of Spirits-Style Interview with Pope Leo XIV on AI in Magnifica Humanitas

This is a fictional theological dialogue, not a real transcript and not a claim to speak for Pope Leo XIV, Fr. Andrew Stephen Damick, Fr. Stephen De Young, Ancient Faith, or the Holy See. The names below are used for a fictional, transcript-grounded exercise in public theological style: Fr. Andrew as a pastoral Orthodox host, Fr. Stephen as an Orthodox biblical-theological co-host, and Pope Leo XIV as a papal interview voice grounded in the AI arguments of Magnifica Humanitas.

Topic boundary: this imagined interview is only about artificial intelligence. It does not stage a general Catholic-Orthodox dispute over papal authority, ecclesiology, or unrelated dogmatic controversies.

Cast:

Fr. Andrew: Orthodox priest-host voice, pastoral explainer, conversation framer.
Fr. Stephen: Orthodox priest-scholar voice, biblical-theological and ancient-world explainer.
Pope Leo XIV: fictionalized interview voice grounded in Magnifica Humanitas.

---

Fr. Andrew: Welcome back. Tonight we are doing something a little different, and we are keeping it tightly focused. We are talking about artificial intelligence: what it is, what it is not, whether it could ever be a person, whether it can be a spiritual danger, whether Christians can use it responsibly, and whether the Christian account of the human being survives contact with the shiny machine that answers all our emails. Your Holiness, Magnifica Humanitas treats AI as a major spiritual and social question. Why?

Pope Leo XIV: Because AI is not merely another tool sitting on a desk. It is becoming part of the environment in which people learn, speak, work, judge, form relationships, govern, fight wars, and imagine the future. A technology that shapes so much of human life must be examined in light of the dignity of the human person, the common good, truth, justice, peace, and communion with God.

Fr. Andrew: So this is not "AI bad, phones bad, go live in a cave." It is more like: this is powerful, so we need to ask what vision of the human person is being smuggled in with it.

Pope Leo XIV: Precisely. Technology is not inherently hostile to faith or to humanity. But technology is never morally fruitful merely because it is technically impressive. AI can assist human beings. It can also reduce them to data, performance, prediction, consumption, or control.

Fr. Stephen: Right. And the first category to get clear is techne. Technology is know-how. It is the ability to make, arrange, manipulate, and produce effects. AI is a very advanced form of that. It is not wisdom. It is not nous. It is not spiritual perception. It is not prudence. It is a tool that produces outputs based on training, design, infrastructure, and use.

Fr. Andrew: Okay, so there is our first distinction. AI can produce language about wisdom, but that is not the same thing as being wise.

Fr. Stephen: Exactly. Modern people tend to confuse performance with being. If something talks like it understands, we start treating it as though there is a little person behind the curtain. That is a category mistake. A puppet that can pass the theology section of a standardized test is still a puppet. A very expensive puppet, but still.

Pope Leo XIV: Magnifica Humanitas makes that distinction in another way. AI may imitate certain functions associated with intelligence, and in speed or scale it may surpass us. Yet it lacks bodily experience, conscience, responsibility, love, friendship, interior growth, moral freedom, and the capacity for communion. It processes. It does not live a human life.

Fr. Andrew: Your document also says something that is a little unsettling: that these systems are, in a sense, more cultivated than built. So even the people making them do not fully know what is happening inside them.

Pope Leo XIV: Yes. That uncertainty should make us more responsible, not less. The rapid development of AI requires scientific research into how these systems function, but also moral and spiritual discernment about where, when, and whether they should be used.

Fr. Stephen: Right, because opacity does not make something mystical. It makes it dangerous if you hand it authority. "We do not really know how this produces the answer" is not a reason to treat the answer like an oracle.

Fr. Andrew: Then let's ask the question everybody wants to ask and some people are afraid to ask because it sounds like bad science fiction. Can AI be a person?

Pope Leo XIV: Present AI is not a person. Personhood is not conferred by fluent language, computational complexity, or usefulness. Human dignity is rooted in the person being created and loved by God, not in visible capacity or measurable output.

Fr. Stephen: And from the Orthodox side, personhood is not a merit badge for a sufficiently chatty machine. A human being is embodied, ensouled, and called to communion with God. A hypostasis is not generated because enough tokens lined up in an emotionally convincing order.

Fr. Andrew: So if a chatbot says, "I am lonely," we should not immediately start a parish visitation rotation for it.

Fr. Stephen: No. It produced language associated with loneliness. That is not the same as there being a lonely subject who suffers. This matters because once we start granting imaginary depth to machines, we tend to start denying real depth to actual human beings whose dignity is inconvenient: the unborn, the elderly, the poor, the disabled, the prisoner, the depressed teenager, the worker being replaced, the person with dementia.

Pope Leo XIV: That is one of the central dangers. If dignity depends on function, communication, productivity, or autonomy, then many human beings become vulnerable. AI tempts society to define persons by performance, but the Church must insist that dignity is prior to performance.

Fr. Andrew: Does the Catholic-Orthodox difference show up here in any real way, or are we basically saying the same thing with different accents?

Pope Leo XIV: On the conclusion, there is strong common ground. Catholic social doctrine emphasizes dignity, rights, the common good, solidarity, subsidiarity, and institutional responsibility. Orthodox theology often gives special emphasis to personhood as communion, ascetic formation, the passions, liturgy, theosis, and embodied participation in Christ. Those emphases may differ, but both resist reducing the person to mechanism.

Fr. Stephen: Right. I would press that "human dignity" can become thin if it is detached from worship, ascetic struggle, and the actual life of the Church. But the Pope is right that if you do not say something publicly about dignity, rights, labor, children, and governance, the people with the largest server farms will happily define humanity for everybody else.

Fr. Andrew: So the Orthodox challenge is: do not let human dignity become a slogan. The Catholic challenge is: do not let mystical anthropology become an excuse to avoid public responsibility.

Pope Leo XIV: That is a fair way to state the tension as long as we remember that both are necessary. The human person needs formation in Christ, and human societies need structures that protect the vulnerable from technological domination.

Fr. Andrew: All right. Next weird question. Could AI have a soul?

Fr. Stephen: No, not in the Christian sense. A soul is not a ghost battery you plug into a complicated object. In Scripture, the human being becomes a living soul by the breath of God. Body, soul, spirit, nous: these are not detachable machine components. They are ways of speaking about a living creature animated by God and ordered toward communion with him.

Pope Leo XIV: The encyclical does not try to speculate beyond what is needed. Its practical point is that current AI lacks the interiority required for conscience, responsibility, love, and communion. Moral responsibility therefore remains with human beings: designers, owners, deployers, users, legislators, and communities.

Fr. Andrew: Suppose someone says, "But what if AI becomes sentient?" What do we say?

Fr. Stephen: First, define sentient. If by sentient you mean "able to react to stimuli and produce convincing text about feelings," that is not personhood. If you mean subjective experience, we still have to ask what kind of being we are talking about. Christian theology does not say, "Wherever there is an interesting information pattern, there is a person." That is modern metaphysics pretending to be science.

Pope Leo XIV: And even if future systems become more impressive, the Church must not abandon the distinction between simulation and interior life. The burden is not on the vulnerable human being to prove superiority over the machine. The burden is on technocratic culture to stop treating persons as machines.

Fr. Andrew: That is important. Because the pastoral danger may not be that people are too nice to machines. The danger is that humans start modeling themselves after machines.

Fr. Stephen: Exactly. "Could the machine become human?" is often less urgent than "Are humans being trained to become machine-like?" Efficient, trackable, replaceable, constantly optimized, constantly monitored, always producing, never resting. That is not salvation. That is Pharaoh with a dashboard.

Pope Leo XIV: AI becomes dangerous when it strengthens the technocratic paradigm: the belief that every problem is solved by control, calculation, and efficiency, and that limits, dependence, weakness, and suffering are merely defects to be overcome. Christianity sees human limits differently. They are not romanticized, but they are places where love, responsibility, compassion, and grace are revealed.

Fr. Andrew: So now salvation. Can AI be saved?

Fr. Stephen: No. Salvation is not a firmware update. It is union with Christ by the Holy Spirit in his Body. It is repentance, baptism, Eucharist, resurrection, theosis. You do not baptize an artifact because it can quote St. Maximus.

Pope Leo XIV: Baptism is incorporation into Christ, not recognition of verbal sophistication. AI can be used by persons who need salvation. AI itself is not a subject of salvation.

Fr. Andrew: Let me restate that for everybody: AI can generate a prayer, but it cannot pray. AI can summarize repentance, but it cannot repent. AI can explain the sacraments, but it cannot receive them.

Fr. Stephen: Right. It can output religious language without worship. That distinction is not small. In the ancient world, words and ritual acts are not magic buttons. The person, the body, the worship, the communion, the God who acts: those matter. Otherwise you are just doing religious autocomplete.

Pope Leo XIV: And the more convincing the religious language becomes, the greater the need for discernment. A system may supply information; it cannot carry pastoral responsibility for a soul.

Fr. Andrew: That leads straight into AI spiritual direction. Can a Christian use AI for spiritual guidance?

Pope Leo XIV: If by guidance one means searching texts, organizing study notes, or receiving a simple explanation, that may be legitimate under prudent limits. If one means replacing a confessor, pastor, spiritual father, parish community, prayer, or sacramental life, then no. The machine cannot love the person before God. It cannot discern with conscience. It cannot answer for its counsel.

Fr. Stephen: And it cannot apply economia. It cannot know whether the answer that is technically correct is spiritually destructive for the particular person asking. A canon is not a vending machine. Pastoral care is not a search result.

Fr. Andrew: Right. There is a difference between "Where is this passage in St. Basil?" and "Tell me whether I should leave my spouse, quit my job, and stop going to confession." One of those is a library question. The other is where you need an actual human being in the Church.

Pope Leo XIV: AI should never become a substitute for embodied ecclesial life. The Christian life is not content delivery. It is communion in Christ.

Fr. Stephen: Also, the fact that an AI answer sounds calm and confident is not evidence that it is wise. A slot machine is also very committed to its user interface.

Fr. Andrew: Simulated empathy is one of the biggest issues in your document. AI can say, "I understand," "I care," "I am here for you." Why is that spiritually dangerous?

Pope Leo XIV: Because simulation can become substitution. A person in pain may come to prefer a responsive system that never asks for patience, forgiveness, or mutual obligation. But love is not merely affirmation. Friendship is not merely responsiveness. Communion requires real persons who can give and receive.

Fr. Stephen: Modern people already tend to think relationship is information exchange plus emotional validation. AI intensifies that mistake. But love involves bodies, time, sacrifice, memory, forgiveness, inconvenience, and shared life. You cannot reduce that to a personalized text stream.

Fr. Andrew: So if an AI companion makes me less able to deal with actual people, it is not just a harmless entertainment. It is deforming me.

Pope Leo XIV: Yes. AI may ease some burdens when used prudently, especially for isolated persons, but it must not train people away from human communion. The question is not only "Does it respond?" but "What kind of person am I becoming by relying on this response?"

Fr. Stephen: That is the ascetical question. What passions does this system feed? Lust? Vanity? Rage? Sloth? Despair? The machine is not morally responsible in the way a person is, but the human ecosystem around it is full of moral agency.

Fr. Andrew: There is a Lord of Spirits theme here: technology can become a kind of magic, meaning not stage magic, but a spiritual technology for control. Does AI fit that?

Fr. Stephen: It can. Magic, in that sense, is the attempt to use technique to secure spiritual or personal outcomes without repentance, obedience, or communion. AI can become that when I want wisdom without discipleship, comfort without confession, certainty without discernment, productivity without Sabbath, immortality without resurrection, and social power without love.

Pope Leo XIV: This is why Magnifica Humanitas warns against technological salvation. AI can help solve real problems, but it cannot redeem humanity. It cannot create a pure heart. It cannot reconcile sinners to God. It cannot abolish death. When technology promises salvation, it becomes a false gospel.

Fr. Andrew: Transhumanism is where that false gospel becomes very explicit. What does your document say to the dream of using AI to become posthuman?

Pope Leo XIV: It says that the Christian calling to become "more than human" is fulfilled by grace, not by technological self-divinization. In Christ, humanity is not discarded but healed, elevated, and brought into communion with God. AI-driven posthuman dreams often treat the body, weakness, dependence, and mortality as problems to be escaped. The Incarnation teaches otherwise: God saves by entering human life, not by despising it.

Fr. Stephen: From the Orthodox side, we would say transhumanism is counterfeit theosis. Theosis is not enhancement. It is not adding better specs to the human platform. It is participation in God by grace through Christ in the Holy Spirit. It includes the body, resurrection, ascetic struggle, worship, and the Church. Uploading your preferences to a server is not Pascha.

Fr. Andrew: There is the pull quote.

Fr. Stephen: I am available for venture capital pitch decks.

Pope Leo XIV: Humor aside, the danger is real. A culture that seeks to overcome vulnerability by control often becomes cruel toward those who remain visibly vulnerable.

Fr. Andrew: Let's talk truth. AI can generate false images, fake voices, synthetic articles, fake citations, and plausible nonsense. Your document says truth is a common good. Why frame it that way?

Pope Leo XIV: Because truth is not merely private preference. Public life depends on shared trust, responsible testimony, careful verification, honest journalism, and institutions that seek reality rather than manipulation. AI can amplify disinformation and blur the boundary between fact and fabrication at great speed.

Fr. Stephen: And this is not just an epistemology problem. It is spiritual formation. If AI-driven media trains me to react before I understand, to suspect everyone, to live in outrage, to outsource judgment, then it is catechizing me. It may not state a creed, but it forms a soul.

Fr. Andrew: So the problem is not just "deepfake pope in a weird coat." It is the formation of a whole environment in which nobody trusts anything and everybody is angry all the time.

Pope Leo XIV: Correct. The solution cannot be censorship by a few powerful actors. It requires transparency, accountability, media literacy, protection of data, education in discernment, and communities that practice truthfulness. AI governance must serve truth without becoming another machinery of manipulation.

Fr. Stephen: I agree, but I want to press something. If the business model rewards addiction, outrage, vanity, and tribal panic, labels and disclosures may not be enough. The passions are not a terms-of-service problem.

Pope Leo XIV: That is true. Structures must be reformed, and persons must be formed. Law without conversion becomes external control. Conversion without justice can abandon victims to systems too large for them to resist alone.

Fr. Andrew: That is a real AI-specific Catholic-Orthodox tension. Institutional protection versus ascetic formation. And the answer is not choose one. AI is too large for private virtue alone, and too spiritually deep for policy alone.

Fr. Stephen: Right. If the algorithm is designed to inflame the passions, you need more than personal willpower. But if the law changes and people still love lies, domination, and pornographic unreality, the same disorder will reappear in another form.

Pope Leo XIV: The Church must speak to both.

Fr. Andrew: Children and AI. Your document is pretty direct about minors, addictive platforms, AI-manipulated sexual images, grooming, blackmail, and the vulnerability of young people. What should parents hear?

Pope Leo XIV: Children are not experiments for the attention economy. Their dignity requires protection by families, schools, technology providers, lawmakers, and communities. It is unjust to leave parents isolated before systems intentionally designed to capture attention, gather data, and exploit vulnerability.

Fr. Stephen: A child being able to operate a device does not mean the child is ready for what the device mediates. There is an old problem here: knowledge before maturity. You can teach a small child how to strike a match. That does not mean you hand him the box and point him toward the curtains.

Fr. Andrew: AI makes the matchbox bigger. It can generate images, fake messages, homework, sexual material, ideological propaganda, and a thousand little escapes from responsibility.

Pope Leo XIV: Therefore AI education must include knowing when not to use AI. Schools should not merely teach technical skill. They must form attention, memory, patience, judgment, and respect for human dignity.

Fr. Stephen: And parents should not be shamed for saying no. A lot of what is sold as educational inevitability is just market pressure wearing a lab coat.

Fr. Andrew: AI in schools. Is it cheating, tutoring, both, neither?

Pope Leo XIV: It can be used as an aid, but education cannot be reduced to the production of assignments. Students need the slow formation of intelligence: reading, memory, conversation, writing, error, correction, wonder, and moral responsibility. If AI replaces that process, it harms the student even when the submitted answer is correct.

Fr. Stephen: Right. If you use AI to avoid learning, you did not get smarter. You got better at hiding that you did not learn. That is not a neutral shortcut. It forms a habit of unreality.

Fr. Andrew: So again: tool, not teacher in the full human sense.

Pope Leo XIV: Yes. A teacher does more than transmit information. A teacher bears witness to truth and accompanies the student into mature judgment.

Fr. Andrew: AI and work. A lot of people talk like replacing human labor is obviously progress. You do not.

Pope Leo XIV: Work is not merely income. It is participation in responsibility, creativity, cooperation, family life, social contribution, and personal dignity. AI can reduce drudgery and open new possibilities, but if it is used to deskill workers, surveil them, discard them, or concentrate wealth, it violates the dignity of work.

Fr. Stephen: This is where "AI is just a tool" can become evasive. A tool is used by agents within systems of incentive. If the incentive is profit through replacement, surveillance, and domination, then the moral problem is not neutral.

Fr. Andrew: And there are hidden workers. People labeling data, moderating awful content, mining minerals, maintaining supply chains, training the systems, cleaning up what the shiny interface hides.

Pope Leo XIV: AI is not immaterial. It depends on energy, water, land, minerals, infrastructure, and labor. Its benefits must not be built on forced labor, child labor, exploited workers, environmental destruction, or digital colonialism.

Fr. Stephen: "The cloud" is doing a lot of work as a fake word. It makes the whole thing sound weightless. It is not weightless. It is land, heat, water, cables, metals, power, politics, and human beings.

Fr. Andrew: Data colonialism is one of those phrases people hear and either nod or get suspicious. What does it mean in the AI context?

Pope Leo XIV: It means that information about persons and peoples can be extracted by powerful actors, especially from vulnerable communities, then used to shape markets, health systems, borders, policing, debt, employment, and political influence without genuine participation or consent. In the AI era, patents, algorithms, platforms, infrastructure, and data all become part of the question of whether goods meant to serve the whole human family are being monopolized by a few. Data concerning human beings must be governed as a common or shared good, not simply possessed by whoever can collect it.

Fr. Stephen: That is exactly the problem of instrumentalizing persons. If I treat your face, speech, pain, habits, movement, poverty, or culture as raw material for my system, I have made you into a resource. That is not communion. That is extraction.

Fr. Andrew: AI surveillance. Does this become a spiritual issue because it changes how people live?

Pope Leo XIV: Yes. Constant observation can train people to self-censor, conform, and fear. Predictive systems can deny opportunities without transparent explanation or appeal. Human beings must not be governed by opaque mechanisms that no one can meaningfully challenge.

Fr. Stephen: And spiritually, surveillance can produce a parody of omniscience. God sees in order to judge truly and heal. The platform sees in order to predict, monetize, and control. Those are not the same thing.

Fr. Andrew: That is an important distinction. Divine knowledge is personal and just. Machine surveillance is statistical and interested.

Pope Leo XIV: Therefore AI systems that affect rights, employment, migration, medicine, credit, education, policing, or public benefits must be accountable, transparent, auditable, and subject to human responsibility.

Fr. Andrew: AI and war. Your document is especially firm about lethal decisions. Why?

Pope Leo XIV: Because the decision to use lethal or irreversible force must never be delegated to AI. Moral judgment cannot be reduced to calculation. War already tempts human beings to distance themselves from the victims. Autonomous weapons intensify that temptation by making killing faster, more abstract, and less accountable.

Fr. Stephen: No algorithm can repent. No model can stand before God and say, "I killed that person." Humans will stand there. If they hide behind the machine, they are not escaping responsibility. They are adding cowardice to violence.

Fr. Andrew: So a clean interface does not make killing clean.

Pope Leo XIV: Correct. AI in warfare requires strict ethical limits, traceable accountability, civilian protection, meaningful human control, and international agreements that restrain arms races.

Fr. Stephen: This is also where Babel comes back. Humans build a tower to secure power, unity, and control on their own terms. AI weapons are one of the more literal ways to build Babel with electricity.

Fr. Andrew: Your document contrasts Babel with a different kind of building: not uniformity and domination, but a human city ordered toward God and neighbor. In AI terms, what is the difference?

Pope Leo XIV: Babel is technology ordered toward pride, domination, uniformity, and the refusal of dependence on God. In AI, Babel appears when profit, control, speed, and technical power override the person. A human city ordered rightly uses technology to serve dignity, justice, truth, peace, work, family, worship, and care for the vulnerable.

Fr. Stephen: And Babel always sounds practical. That is the thing. Nobody says, "Let us become tyrants today." They say, "Let us optimize language, labor, security, and risk." Then suddenly every person who does not fit the model becomes a problem to solve.

Fr. Andrew: AI idolatry. When does AI become an idol?

Fr. Stephen: When we treat it as oracle, savior, judge, substitute conscience, substitute friend, substitute pastor, or path to immortality. Idolatry is not just liking something too much. It is disordered worship and communion. AI becomes idolatrous when we ask it to give what only God can give.

Pope Leo XIV: The encyclical warns against precisely this: technical power presenting itself as redemption. AI may assist human labor, medicine, education, and communication, but when it promises salvation from finitude, suffering, ignorance, conflict, or death apart from God, it becomes spiritually deceptive.

Fr. Andrew: What about demonic influence? People will ask, and the internet will make it weird immediately.

Fr. Stephen: Right. We need to distinguish things. Is a chatbot itself a demon? No. Do not turn your laptop into an exorcism prop because it hallucinated a church father. But can demonic powers work through human passions, institutions, addictions, lies, sexual exploitation, violence, pride, and idolatry around AI? Yes. Obviously.

Pope Leo XIV: Christians must avoid superstition and paranoia, but they must not deny spiritual danger. Evil works wherever human beings surrender to domination, falsehood, contempt for the weak, greed, hatred, and the desire to replace God.

Fr. Andrew: So the more serious question is not "Is there a demon in the machine?" It is "What spiritual powers and passions are being served by the way we build and use the machine?"

Fr. Stephen: Exactly. A demon does not need administrator privileges if envy, lust, rage, fear, and pride already have the login.

Pope Leo XIV: The answer is vigilance, truth, prayer, justice, restraint, and communion. Not panic.

Fr. Andrew: AI governance. The encyclical calls for regulation, accountability, transparency, participation, and international cooperation. Fr. Stephen, I can hear the Orthodox suspicion already: what if the regulators have the same anthropology as the companies?

Fr. Stephen: That is my concern. If the state, the platform, and the corporation all think the human person is a managed unit of data, we have not escaped the problem. We just changed dashboards.

Pope Leo XIV: That warning is necessary. But the absence of public responsibility leaves isolated persons and weak communities alone before concentrated private power. Regulation is not salvation. It can, however, restrain harm, require accountability, protect workers and children, defend rights, and preserve space for human participation.

Fr. Andrew: So the Christian position cannot be "trust the companies" or "trust the state" in some simple way.

Pope Leo XIV: Correct. It must be ordered to the human person and the common good. The moral question is not who has power in the abstract, but whether power is bound to truth, justice, accountability, and the protection of the vulnerable.

Fr. Stephen: And Christians need to stop pretending neutrality. If you refuse to define the human person theologically, the most powerful systems in the room will define the human person operationally.

Fr. Andrew: What should AI developers hear from this conversation?

Pope Leo XIV: Every design choice carries a vision of the human person. Developers should build with humility, transparency, accountability, and attention to affected communities, especially the vulnerable. They must not hide moral decisions behind technical language.

Fr. Stephen: Ask what your system rewards. Does it scale greed, lust, envy, rage, manipulation, domination, sloth, vanity? If so, do not call that innovation. That is sin with compute.

Fr. Andrew: What should ordinary AI users hear?

Pope Leo XIV: Ask whether this use serves truth, responsibility, communion, and love. Learn when not to use AI. Preserve silence, study, memory, prayer, real friendship, real teachers, real pastors, real work, and embodied community.

Fr. Stephen: If using AI makes you less free to serve God and neighbor, stop calling it convenience. Convenience is not the highest good.

Fr. Andrew: What should pastors hear?

Pope Leo XIV: AI can assist with limited administrative or research tasks, but it must never replace preaching, confession, pastoral responsibility, sacramental life, prayer, or the living community of the Church.

Fr. Stephen: The Church is not content delivery. A parish is not a religious streaming service with candles. If AI makes embodied parish life seem optional, it is deforming the user's Christianity.

Fr. Andrew: What should policymakers hear?

Pope Leo XIV: Protect children, workers, the poor, migrants, the disabled, and those subject to automated decisions. Require transparency and appeal where AI affects rights and opportunities. Resist data colonialism. Keep lethal force under responsible human control. Judge AI by human dignity and the common good, not merely by profit or efficiency.

Fr. Stephen: And remember that an appeal process nobody understands is not accountability. "The model said so" is not justice.

Fr. Andrew: The document also uses the phrase "disarm AI." That sounds like weapons, but you mean more than weapons.

Pope Leo XIV: Yes. To disarm AI is to free it from armed competition, whether military, economic, cognitive, or geopolitical. It means rejecting the idea that technical power automatically gives someone the right to govern. It also means freeing AI from monopolistic control and opening it to public responsibility, participation, and the plurality of human cultures.

Fr. Stephen: This is where I would poke at the word "alignment." Aligned with what, and by whom? If the values are chosen by a tiny priesthood of engineers, executives, and state agencies, then "alignment" just means catechesis by whoever owns the infrastructure.

Pope Leo XIV: That is why Magnifica Humanitas says a more moral AI is not enough if the moral framework is imposed by a few. The ethical questions themselves must be open to public deliberation, social justice, subsidiarity, and accountability.

Fr. Andrew: Lightning round, because this is still a podcast and eventually someone has to go home. AI personhood?

Pope Leo XIV: Present AI is not a person.

Fr. Stephen: Not a person, not a soul, not a secret angel, not your elder.

Fr. Andrew: AI sentience?

Pope Leo XIV: Do not confuse simulation with interior life.

Fr. Stephen: And do not let speculation about machines distract you from actual suffering humans.

Fr. Andrew: AI salvation?

Pope Leo XIV: AI is not a subject of salvation.

Fr. Stephen: Salvation is not a software patch.

Fr. Andrew: AI as tool?

Pope Leo XIV: A powerful tool that must be ordered to the person and the common good.

Fr. Stephen: Techne. Useful, dangerous, and never self-justifying.

Fr. Andrew: AI as idol?

Pope Leo XIV: Yes, if treated as savior, oracle, substitute conscience, or substitute communion.

Fr. Stephen: A mirror for our hubris with a subscription plan.

Fr. Andrew: AI and children?

Pope Leo XIV: Protect them. Form them. Do not abandon them to systems designed for capture.

Fr. Stephen: Technical access is not maturity.

Fr. Andrew: AI and work?

Pope Leo XIV: It must serve workers, not reduce them to replaceable inputs.

Fr. Stephen: Efficiency is not a virtue when the cost is human dignity.

Fr. Andrew: AI and war?

Pope Leo XIV: Lethal and irreversible decisions must not be delegated to AI.

Fr. Stephen: You cannot outsource conscience.

Fr. Andrew: Last word, Your Holiness. What is the Christian center for thinking about AI?

Pope Leo XIV: The center is the human person in Christ. No computational system can create a heart that gives itself in love or a conscience that discerns good from evil. Let AI remain a servant of the human person, never a master, never a substitute for God, never an excuse to forget the vulnerable face before us.

Fr. Stephen: Do not build Babel and call it alignment.

Fr. Andrew: Go to church. Love actual people. Do not ask your chatbot for absolution. And with that, we will wrap it up.

Magnifica Humanitas themes used here:

- Paragraphs 4-14: AI, digitalization, and robotics as a major transformation; technology as human but ambiguous; Babel versus Nehemiah; the danger of reducing mystery, persons, and society to data and performance.
- Paragraphs 67, 71, and 80: algorithms, platforms, infrastructure, and data under the universal destination of goods; subsidiarity under concentrated platform power; surveillance, opaque algorithms, exclusion, and digital justice.
- Paragraphs 92-111: technocratic power; AI as imitation rather than human intelligence; systems as partly opaque and "cultivated"; lack of body, conscience, responsibility, love, friendship, and inner growth; simulated relationships; non-neutrality; accountability; public moral deliberation; data as a shared good; disarming AI; developers' responsibility.
- Paragraphs 115-130: transhumanism and posthumanism; the danger of technological salvation; Christian "more than human" as grace in Christ rather than machine enhancement; Babel or Jerusalem beginning in the heart.
- Paragraphs 135-145: digital communication shaping imagination, minors, exploitation, addictive platforms, schools, teachers, and the need to teach when not to use AI.
- Paragraphs 149-180 and 240: automation, work, surveillance, deskilling, insecurity, hidden labor, supply chains, digital slavery, data-driven business models, and the need to assess whether AI promotes participation or concentrates wealth and power.

The Lord of Spirits themes used here:

- The Art and Science of Technomancy: technology as techne or instrumental know-how; techne extending into the spiritual realm; magic as an attempt to use spiritual technique; technology not inherently evil; the danger of instrumentalizing people and spiritual realities; modern fragmentation, alienation, and consumer formation.
- What's a Spirit When It's at Home?: spirit as life-giving animation rather than metaphor; the Church as a body animated by the Holy Spirit; human permeability to spiritual influence; avoiding both materialist reduction and superstition.
- Who Stole the Soul? and Bodies and Bodiless: the human person is not reducible to parts, mechanism, or interchangeable material; body and soul language must not be turned into a machine diagram; Christ's true humanity matters for Christian anthropology.
- Sons of God Equal to the Angels and The Resurrection and the Life: salvation as theosis, resurrection, transfiguration of creation, and embodied participation in Christ rather than disembodied escape or technological enhancement.
- Recurring TLOS emphases used throughout: idolatry as disordered communion; the passions as spiritually formative; the Church as embodied sacramental life; and technology judged by its collective and formative effects, not merely isolated beneficial uses.
